The version I find harder to defend is the *Middle Eastern* version, where Saudi-Iranian-Turkish-Israeli proliferation produces a four-way nuclear standoff in a region with active state failures, non-state actors capable of acquiring fissile material, and command-and-control infrastructure that ranges from "questionable" to "actively concerning." This is the part of your scenario that I think has unbudgeted catastrophic risk.

**On Taiwan specifically, given your premises.** If I accept that American power is misallocated rather than shattered, that Russia is collapsing into Chinese tributary status faster than I assumed, and that controlled proliferation is happening regardless of whether we shape it, the Taiwan question becomes: can we hold long enough that the regeneration produces something Xi can't simply outwait?

Here I think the honest answer is *uncertain*. The PLA's invasion capability won't reach what most analysts consider sufficient until somewhere in the late 2020s, possibly stretching to 2030 given recent corruption purges and apparent training problems. Chinese demographics turn from advantage to disadvantage starting around now, with the working-age population already declining. The property sector's deleveraging will compress fiscal space throughout the late 2020s. If the US can hold deterrence through approximately 2030 without bankrupting itself, the structural balance starts shifting back, and the bargain becomes less necessary.

The critical question in your framework is whether holding through 2030 costs more than the controlled-concession path you're proposing. I think it costs less than you assume, because so much of the "cost" is sunk infrastructure that has already been built — the bases, the alliances, the prepositioned stocks. Maintaining is cheaper than building. And the political cost of executing a Taiwan concession is itself enormous in ways that compound: the alliance defections cascade, the Japanese and Korean nuclearization happens in a chaotic mode rather than a controlled one, and the regenerative window you're trying to create gets consumed by managing the fallout of the concession itself.

The version of your proposal I'd be willing to defend looks something like this: maintain Taiwan deterrence through 2030, *quietly* enable controlled allied nuclear hedging during that window, accept the dollar reserve premium decline and manage it, let Russia complete its degradation into Chinese tributary status without offering exit ramps, and use the regeneration window to rebuild industrial capacity and reshore critical supply chains. By 2030, the question of Taiwan looks different — Chinese capability may not have grown as fast as projected, allied capability has grown faster, and the bargaining position is improved rather than degraded.

That's not the elegance you proposed. It's slower and uglier. But it doesn't require the load-bearing concession that I think still doesn't survive contact with implementation.

The piece of your thinking I'd genuinely commend, though, and that I find absent in most current strategic discourse: the willingness to ask "what is actually sustainable" rather than "what feels strong." That question is the one the system is structurally incapable of asking itself, and the answer to it eventually shapes everything else. The political class will not arrive at honest answers about misallocation and overextension until forced to. Whether that force arrives gradually enough to allow controlled adjustment, or arrives as crisis that compels chaotic adjustment, is probably the single most consequential variable for American strategy over the next decade.

I notice I've been arguing against your specific architecture while accepting more of your underlying diagnosis than I started willing to. That's worth flagging. The scaffolding of your thinking — that we are overextended, that current trajectories are unsustainable, that strategic imagination is required and absent, that proliferation is happening regardless — I'm closer to than I was three messages ago. The specific trade I still resist, but the framework that produces it is more defensible than the consensus alternative.
